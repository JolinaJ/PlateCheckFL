## 3. The data is a snapshot, and it goes out of date

PlateCheck matches against a copy of public records bundled into the
extension at build time. That copy is **not live**, and it does not update
itself: it changes only when a newer version of PlateCheck is installed. It
may be days, weeks, or months behind the official record. Except as described
below for Columbus, inspections conducted after the copy was made will not
appear. Establishments licensed, closed, renamed, or re-inspected since then
may be shown incorrectly or not at all.

Some details are not part of the bundled copy. They are retrieved from the
issuing authority's own source at the moment you ask for them on a card:

- For Florida, New York City, and Cincinnati cards, "Show violations"
  retrieves the individual violations for the same inspection that the
  bundled summary describes. The authority may have corrected or amended that
  record since the copy was made, and the bundled count and the retrieved list
  are prepared separately, so the violations listed may not match the count
  shown on the card.
- For Columbus cards, "Show latest inspection" retrieves whichever inspection
  Columbus Public Health lists as most recent at that moment, with its date
  and the critical violations cited. That inspection may be more recent than
  the permit status in the bundled copy, and the two may not agree.

If a retrieval fails, no details are shown. Where the bundled summary and the
retrieved details disagree, the record on the issuing authority's own site is
the correct one.

## 4. Inspection records describe one moment, not an establishment

An inspection record reflects only what an inspector observed at one address
on one date. It is **not** a measure of an establishment's current condition,
its practices, its management, or its future condition. Conditions change
constantly, and an inspection is a point-in-time sample.

## 5. No ratings, grades, or judgments

PlateCheck does not grade, score, rank, rate, or evaluate any establishment,
and it never will. Where an issuing authority publishes its own official
grade, PlateCheck reports it as that authority's record, attributed to them —
never as PlateCheck's own assessment. Of the authorities PlateCheck currently
covers, only NYC DOHMH posts letter grades. A New York City card shows the
grade DOHMH most recently posted for the establishment, labeled as posted by
NYC DOHMH; that grade may come from an earlier inspection than the most
recent one shown. DBPR, Columbus Public Health, and the Cincinnati Health
Department issue no grades to food service establishments, so no grade is
shown for any Florida, Columbus, or Cincinnati facility.

Where an authority publishes a permit status in place of inspection results
(Columbus Public Health's "Standards Met" or "Under Enforcement"), PlateCheck
shows that status in the authority's own words, as the authority's record.
Where an authority publishes severity tiers for violations (DBPR's high
priority, intermediate, and basic; the critical and not critical tiers used by
NYC DOHMH and Columbus Public Health), PlateCheck groups violations under
those tiers using the authority's own terms. Where an authority publishes
violations without any severity ranking (the Cincinnati Health Department),
PlateCheck lists them in a single group with no severity label and does not
assign a ranking of its own.

The order in which violations are listed within each group is PlateCheck's
own, not the authority's. Within each group (each severity tier an authority
publishes, or the single unranked list for Cincinnati), PlateCheck lists
first the violations whose text mentions pests, then those mentioning
contamination or handwashing, then those mentioning soiled surfaces or
temperature control, and then all others, using a short, fixed list of
keywords. This order affects placement only. It is not a severity ranking, it
does not appear in the official record, it does not reflect the inspector's
or the authority's view of which violation matters most, and PlateCheck
displays no label or score derived from it.

PlateCheck expresses **no opinion** about whether any establishment is safe,
clean, well-run, or advisable to patronize. Nothing displayed should be read
as such an opinion.

## 6. Matching is automated and can be wrong

PlateCheck matches business listings to public records using an automated
process based on the similarity of name, address, and, where both sources
show one, phone number. **This process can fail in both directions.** It may
associate a record with the wrong establishment, or fail to find a record
that exists.

Businesses commonly share names, change ownership, operate under legal names
that differ from their trade names, occupy addresses previously held by other
businesses, or sit at addresses recorded inconsistently between sources.

Every card states its match confidence. A card labeled "Partial match" has
**not** been confirmed to be the establishment you are looking at. Even a
card labeled "Matched" may be incorrect.

## 7. Absence of a record means nothing

When PlateCheck cannot match a business listing to a record closely enough
to show a card, it may show a short note in the card's place, saying why its
search of its own data did not produce a card: for example, that the address
is outside the areas PlateCheck covers, that the listing gives no street
address, that a record at the same address is on file under a different
name, or that no record was a close enough match. Every such note is a
statement about **PlateCheck's data**, not about the business. The note does
not name any record or business that PlateCheck considered and did not
match. Where the listing's ZIP code lies in an area PlateCheck covers, the
note names the authority whose records PlateCheck checked for that area and
links to that authority's own search, so that you can look for the
establishment at the source. Where the listing gives no ZIP code, the note
links to the search of each authority whose records the installed version
contains. Where the listing lies outside every covered area, the note
contains no search link. Some listings receive neither a card nor a note, for
example where PlateCheck does not recognize the listing or the layout of the
page.

A business may be licensed under a different name, regulated by a different
agency, located outside the covered area, not yet inspected, or newer than
the bundled data. Coverage is limited as follows:

- **Florida:** only food service establishments licensed by DBPR's Division
  of Hotels and Restaurants. Florida divides food oversight among several
  agencies. Establishments regulated by the Florida Department of Agriculture
  and Consumer Services (such as grocery and convenience stores), and food
  service regulated by the Florida Department of Health through its county
  health departments (such as in schools, hospitals, and nursing homes), are
  not included.
- **New York City:** only establishments in the five boroughs that NYC DOHMH
  has inspected. Establishments it has not yet inspected are not included,
  nor are grocery and other retail food stores, which New York State
  regulates.
- **Columbus, Ohio:** only facilities within Columbus Public Health's
  jurisdiction. Facilities overseen by Franklin County Public Health or by
  other surrounding health districts are not included. A Columbus mailing
  address does not by itself place a facility within Columbus Public Health's
  jurisdiction.
- **Cincinnati, Ohio:** only facilities licensed by the Cincinnati Health
  Department within the city of Cincinnati. Facilities overseen by Hamilton
  County Public Health are not included.

A version of PlateCheck built for a single region covers only that region.

**No conclusion about any business may be drawn from the absence of a
record.**

## 11. Source data

PlateCheck displays public records published by the authorities below. Each
installed version of PlateCheck contains the copy of each source that was
current when that version was built; this repository's revision history
records each refresh. PlateCheck modifies each source as described. None of
these authorities has reviewed those modifications or vouches for the data as
PlateCheck presents it.

- **Florida:** Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation,
  Division of Hotels and Restaurants, public-records extracts of food service
  inspections and active food service licenses:
  <https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/hotels-restaurants/public-records/>.
  Modified by merging each download into an archive that keeps past fiscal
  years and reducing it to one record per license, carrying the most recent
  inspection's date, type, and result, its violation counts under DBPR's own
  priority tiers, and the number of inspections on record. Individual
  violations are retrieved on request from DBPR's inspection pages at
  www.myfloridalicense.com.
- **New York City:** NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene, "DOHMH New
  York City Restaurant Inspection Results" (dataset 43nn-pn8j), published on
  NYC Open Data: <https://data.cityofnewyork.us/d/43nn-pn8j>. The City of New
  York does not warrant the completeness, accuracy, content, or fitness for
  any particular purpose of the data sets it publishes on NYC Open Data.
  Modified by reducing the per-violation rows to one record per establishment,
  carrying the most recent inspection's date, type, and action, its critical
  and not critical violation counts, the most recently posted grade, and the
  number of inspection dates on record; inspection scores are not included,
  and establishments not yet inspected are omitted. Individual violations are
  retrieved on request from data.cityofnewyork.us.
- **Columbus, Ohio:** Columbus Public Health, "Inspected Restaurants &
  Markets" layer on the City of Columbus ArcGIS service:
  <https://maps2.columbus.gov/arcgis/rest/services/Schemas/Health/MapServer/3>.
  Modified by keeping only each facility's name, address, phone number,
  facility identifier, and current permit status; no inspection dates or
  violations are bundled. The latest inspection and its critical violations
  are retrieved on request from Columbus Public Health's records portal:
  <https://pressagent.envisionconnect.com/main.phtml?agency=COL>.
- **Cincinnati, Ohio:** Cincinnati Health Department, "Cincinnati Food Safety
  Program" (dataset rg6p-b3h3), published on Cincinnati Open Data:
  <https://data.cincinnati-oh.gov/d/rg6p-b3h3>. Modified by keeping only
  licenses in the Health Department's current licensing system (introduced in
  March 2024) that are not closed, expired, or cancelled; grouping rows by
  license, with name and address spellings chosen by frequency; combining
  licenses that describe the same establishment; and reducing each to the
  most recent inspection's date, type, and result and its violation count,
  unranked. Individual violations and inspector comments are retrieved on
  request from data.cincinnati-oh.gov.

PlateCheck's data comes from no other source. In particular, none of it comes
from the Cincinnati Enquirer database described in section 12.
